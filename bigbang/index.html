What the comparison means

The Big Bang model describes the universe evolving from an early hot, dense state. It does not, by itself, prove that this was the absolute beginning of time or decide the universe’s future. In this comparison, the left-hand scenario assumes expansion continues; the right-hand scenario assumes an eternal sequence of expansions and contractions.

A repeating universe requires more than a collapse: it needs a mechanism for a bounce. Neither a previous cycle nor a future bounce has been established by observations. The repeated minimum in this animation represents that assumption, not a simulation of known physics at a singularity.

The irregular galaxy field represents a patch of space, with no depicted outer edge. The layout is illustrative; it is not a map of actual galaxies or a simulation of cosmic structure formation. Expansion and contraction affect the whole patch, rather than spreading from a central explosion. The selected observer stays centred for convenience. The continued-expansion view starts after inflation with a glowing, opaque plasma. Neutral atoms form around 380,000 years after the Big Bang, allowing the background radiation to travel freely. The universe cools through the dark ages before the first stars and galaxies form. Screen colours illustrate cooling and are not calculated temperatures. The observer marker is a reference location, not an object present in the early universe. Galaxy icons appear only later and fade during the cyclic model’s final compression; their detailed formation and destruction are not simulated. Bound galaxies keep a fixed icon size while their separations change.

These are schematic scale-factor animations, not numerical solutions for our universe. The curves and equal cycle lengths are chosen to make the contrast clear; no realistic time scale is implied. Expansion on the left becomes visually faster over the displayed interval. Relative spacing uses the maximum spacing of the first cyclic expansion as a shared reference.
