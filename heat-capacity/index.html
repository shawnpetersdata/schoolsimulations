How the model works

Each simulated thermometer is updated by an energy balance: temperature change = net energy transferred ÷ thermal capacity (ΔT = PnetΔt / mc). Energy moves between each material, its nearby air, and the room. Water also has a simplified, temperature-dependent evaporation loss.

Both samples have a mass of 250 g and absorb 20 W from their lamps while heating. The model uses approximate specific heat capacities of 830 J/(kg·°C) for dry sand and 4,186 J/(kg·°C) for water. The room stays at 21°C.

Air readings include a simplified local thermal response. The airflow, humidity, surface area, container properties and lamp absorption are not measured or independently simulated. Results illustrate energy-transfer principles; they are not predictions for a particular classroom apparatus.

How the lakeshore model works

The day lasts 16 hours (6:00 AM to 10:00 PM), followed by 8 hours of night. Sunlight rises and falls smoothly over daylight hours. The scene is illustrative, not a geographic weather forecast.

Unlike the equal-mass classroom samples, the shore model represents one square metre of beach surface and lake surface. Sand represents a shallow heated layer (12 cm), whereas the lake represents a much deeper 10 m effective mixed layer. The large water thermal mass allows warming to accumulate gradually over weeks rather than following every afternoon. This is a simplified mixed-layer assumption, not a claim that sunlight directly heats water uniformly to 10 m. Each surface gains solar energy and exchanges heat with its local air, the background environment, and deeper material. Water includes simplified evaporative cooling.

Both air probes track nearby thermal conditions through a simplified exchange model. Wind, cloud cover, mixing, currents and exact local weather are not resolved. The numerical results are illustrative and should not be treated as measured beach or lake temperatures.

How the seasonal model works

This is a conceptual comparison of two fictional towns at the same northern latitude, one far inland and one beside a large body of water. Their daily-average temperatures are calculated using the same seasonal energy input, with different thermal response times.

Ground and water temperatures relax toward a changing seasonal energy-balance temperature. Land responds relatively quickly; the large water mass responds over many weeks. The inland air is influenced mainly by nearby land, while shoreline air is influenced by both regional conditions and the slower-changing water. The model represents thermal inertia and marine moderation, not a particular Canadian town or weather forecast.

Clouds, winds, storms, ocean currents, freezing and thawing, and day-to-day weather variability are not individually simulated. Illustrated seasons and temperatures are approximate. The graph uses daily averages rather than daytime highs or overnight lows.
